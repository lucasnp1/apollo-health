Testosterone tells the bone marrow to make more red cells. That is useful up to a point and a problem past it. Hematocrit is the share of your blood that is red cells, and on TRT it is the number most likely to send you back to the doctor.

## Where the lines are

The lab range tops out around 50 percent. Guidelines for TRT say act at 52 to 54. Experienced users keep it under 52 because the climb is slow and steady, and it is easier to hold a number than to chase it down. Over 54, blood is thick enough that clot and stroke risk are real and most clinics pause the protocol.

## Why yours is climbing

- **Dose and peaks.** Bigger weekly doses and big infrequent injections push more red cells than the same total split across the week.
- **Draw timing.** A peak draw reads higher than a trough draw.
- **Dehydration.** Hematocrit is a concentration. A dry morning adds a point or two that is not real.
- **Sleep apnea, smoking, altitude.** Low oxygen tells the marrow to make more cells regardless of testosterone. Loud snorers on TRT should get checked; apnea alone can add three points.

## What people usually do

1. **Retest at trough, well hydrated,** before doing anything. A surprising number of "high" hematocrits are a dry draw day.
2. **Donate blood.** One donation drops hematocrit three to four points within days. Therapeutic phlebotomy through a doctor is the same thing with a prescription.
3. **Split the dose.** Twice or three times a week at the same total lowers the peaks that drive red-cell production.
4. **Watch ferritin.** Every donation takes iron with it. Ferritin under 50 feels like flat energy and poor recovery. Space donations eight weeks apart, take iron with vitamin C between them, and do not donate on a low ferritin.
5. **Bro-science corner.** Grapefruit juice (naringin) and nattokinase circulate as fixes for hematocrit and viscosity. Neither has solid evidence. Hydration, donation and dose frequency do the work.

## The trend matters more than the number

A hematocrit of 51 that was 45 six months ago on the same dose is a different story from a hematocrit of 51 that has sat there for two years. Keep every draw on one line and read the slope. When it keeps climbing on a stable dose, the dose is the lever.
