Testosterone tells the bone marrow to make more red cells. That is useful up to a point and a problem past it. Hematocrit is the share of your blood that is red cells, and on TRT it is the number most likely to send you back to the doctor.

## Where the lines are

The lab range tops out around 50 percent. The Endocrine Society 2018 testosterone guideline treats a hematocrit above 54 percent as a reason to stop or adjust therapy. Lower lines, such as 52 percent, are also commonly quoted, and some clinics use them. Thicker blood is linked to a higher risk of clots, which is why the number is watched so closely.

## Why it climbs

- **Dose and peaks.** Bigger weekly doses and big infrequent injections push more red cells than the same total spread across the week.
- **Draw timing.** A peak draw reads higher than a trough draw.
- **Dehydration.** Hematocrit is a concentration, so a dry morning can read a point or two higher than it really is.
- **Sleep apnea, smoking, altitude.** Low oxygen tells the marrow to make more cells regardless of testosterone. Sleep apnea is commonly quoted as adding a few points on its own.

## What doctors look at

When hematocrit is high, clinicians usually read it next to hemoglobin and RBC (to tell real red-cell growth from a dry draw day), next to ferritin (iron stores), and next to your dose, injection schedule and blood pressure. Blood donation or a therapeutic phlebotomy, and changes to the protocol, are options a doctor may discuss. Which one, if any, is their call with you.

## Things to raise with your doctor

- When the draw was taken relative to your last injection, and whether you were well hydrated.
- Your current dose and injection schedule, and any recent change.
- Snoring, waking up tired or other signs of sleep apnea.
- Your ferritin, if you have given blood before.

## The trend matters more than the number

A hematocrit of 51 that was 45 six months ago on the same dose is a different story from a hematocrit of 51 that has sat there for two years. Keeping every draw on one line, next to your doses, shows the slope, and that is what your doctor will want to see.
